Altis AI Ski Goggles

A full venture business plan for Altis, an AI-powered, safety-first ski goggle that consolidates avalanche beacon, crash detection, SOS, and performance tracking into a single wearable. Built with a five-person team for a venture strategy course; I served as Chief Marketing Officer.

Chief Marketing Officer
Team

The idea

Backcountry skiers heading into high-risk terrain typically carry several separate safety tools — an avalanche beacon, a crash detection device, a communication system — on top of their regular gear. Our team's premise was that this complexity itself is a safety problem: more devices means more friction in an emergency. Altis is a wearable technology concept that folds those functions into a single AI-powered ski goggle, positioned as the first product to combine safety-critical technology with performance tracking in one system.

We scoped Altis around a specific beachhead: experienced backcountry skiers aged 25–45, skiing 20+ days a season, with the income and safety-consciousness to treat gear as an investment rather than an expense. The ski equipment market is roughly $16.5B globally, and wearable sports tech is projected to grow from $94B to over $120B by 2031 — Altis was designed to sit at the intersection of both.

My role: go-to-market & marketing

As CMO, I led the marketing plan and go-to-market strategy — how Altis would actually reach its first customers and build a credible brand in a community that trusts firsthand experience over advertising. A few of the calls I made with the team:

  • A hybrid distribution model — direct-to-consumer to protect margins and collect customer data early, paired with selective partnerships with specialty backcountry retailers for third-party credibility.
  • A founder-led sales motion in Year 1 to keep the brand's technical credibility intact, deliberately avoiding mass-market retail (like Dick's Sporting Goods) where staff couldn't speak to the safety features.
  • Customer acquisition through influencer partnerships with backcountry athletes, on-mountain pop-up demos at Colorado resorts, and a DTC pre-order campaign that doubled as early capital and early-adopter feedback.
  • Retention through owned content — avalanche safety and backcountry decision-making education distributed on Instagram and YouTube — reinforcing the safety-first brand identity rather than relying on paid ads.

We priced Altis at $649.99, positioned against the $700–$1,200 a skier would otherwise spend across separate devices, and validated the approach through expert interviews with staff at Crystal Ski Rental in Boulder and Aspen Snowmass Resort.

Financials & funding

The plan modeled five years of growth from 250 units sold in Year 1 to 15,000 by Year 5 — revenue scaling from about $162K to $9.75M, with gross margin improving from 15.5% to 66.8% as manufacturing volume and supplier terms improved. The company breaks even in Year 4.

  • Funding ask: $3.0M total — $2.8M in staged equity (founders, pre-seed, Series A, bridge) plus a $200K short-term note, covering operating losses through Year 3.
  • Use of funds: ~43% R&D, ~33% G&A, ~21% sales & marketing, ~5% CapEx.
  • Path to profitability: cumulative losses of roughly $2.4M before turning profitable, with $1.41M in net earnings by Year 4 and $3.16M by Year 5.

Team & operations

Altis was built by a five-person founding team, each covering a core function: CEO, CFO, COO, Chief Product Officer, and myself as CMO. We planned to outsource hardware manufacturing to contract partners in sports eyewear and domestic PCB assembly, operate out of a shared Boulder makerspace early on, and hire embedded systems engineers as the first critical technical gap to fill — none of us came in with hardware engineering backgrounds, which shaped a lot of the operational plan.

Reflection

This was my first time owning the marketing and go-to-market strategy for a full venture plan rather than a single feature or interface. It pushed me to think about brand and distribution as design decisions in their own right — deciding where not to sell was as strategic as deciding where to sell. It also gave me a much sharper sense of how customer research, pricing, and channel strategy have to reinforce each other, not just live in separate slides.
